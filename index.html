Course Project

Chronicle:
Collaborative History Annotation

Instructional Design AI & EdTech
Visit Live Site

Overview

What Chronicle Does

Chronicle is a web-based collaborative history annotation platform designed for K–12 students. Rather than reading historical texts alone, students annotate directly on the page — highlighting passages, leaving sticky-note comments, and responding to peers' interpretations in real time.

To deepen engagement, Chronicle offers an Immersion Mode that overlays AI-generated historical backgrounds onto the reading interface. An inline AI tutor answers contextual questions as students read — not by lecturing, but by prompting further inquiry.

The standout feature is Era Chat: students choose a historical witness and converse with an AI-powered character speaking from that lived perspective. A Teacher Dashboard surfaces engagement patterns and AI-synthesized summaries of class annotations.

Learning Theory

What Guides the Design

Theory 01

Vygotsky's Social Constructivism

Knowledge is co-constructed through social interaction. Students annotate to externalize thinking, respond to peers to build shared meaning, and engage with an AI tutor as a "more knowledgeable other."

Theory 02

Flavell's Metacognitive Learning Theory

Learners improve when they monitor and regulate their own thinking. Through annotation and AI-guided questioning, students identify gaps in understanding and develop habits of self-directed historical inquiry.

Features

Key Features

Collaborative Annotation Students highlight passages and leave sticky-note comments inline. Peers' annotations appear alongside the text in real time.
AI Response / Summary / Bot Auto-generated discussion summaries, AI smart-replies, and a dedicated Q&A bot that nudges students to find evidence in the article.
Era Chat — AI Historical Figures Students converse directly with an AI-powered character speaking from a lived historical perspective.
Teacher Dashboard Participation data, pinning tools, and AI-synthesized class summaries for responsive, data-informed instruction.

Automation Assistant:
AI Content Workflow

Workflow Design AI & EdTech
Visit Site * Site is in Chinese & may require login

Overview

From Screenshot
to Structured Table — in Seconds

Teachers shouldn't have to retype what already exists. At Huojian AI Education, every content update meant manually transcribing vocabulary lists, grammar points, and lesson topics from textbook screenshots into spreadsheets — a slow, error-prone process repeated across 115 courses.

This tool eliminates that bottleneck entirely. Built on the Coze platform, it takes a screenshot as input and outputs a fully structured, ready-to-use content table — no reformatting, no retyping, no manual steps in between. The result: roughly a 5× increase in content production efficiency, with fewer transcription errors and less downstream QA overhead.

Design Decision

Why This Approach

The core question wasn't what tool to use — it was where the friction actually was. After observing the content production workflow, the pain point was clear: the gap between visual content (screenshots) and structured data (tables) was being bridged entirely by hand, every single time.

The decision to build an AI-powered automation workflow rather than improve a manual template came from one principle: if the information is already in the image, the system should be able to read it. Three separate workflows were designed to match the three most common teacher tasks — each scoped narrowly on purpose.

How It Works

Three Workflows

01

Word Generation

Upload a vocabulary screenshot. The workflow parses each word, extracts definitions and usage, and populates a multi-dimensional vocabulary table automatically.

02

Syntax Generation

Upload a grammar knowledge slide. Image recognition identifies key rules and examples, outputting a structured grammar reference table ready for the course system.

03

Text Topic Generation

Upload a textbook page. The workflow extracts lesson topics and organizes them into a dimensional table — turning a page of text into navigable, structured content.

Overview

Why This Research

China's Rural Revitalization Strategy places women at the center of agricultural modernization. As urbanization has reshaped the countryside, rural women — particularly in Xinjiang — have become the primary resident population of many villages, yet their entrepreneurial potential remains underutilized.

Our team of 10 — two minority students from Xinjiang and eight Han students from Shanghai — shared a common goal: to understand what actually prevents rural women from starting businesses, and what conditions make success possible. We investigated two sites: Tachaqir Town, Heshuo County and Tumxuk City.

Team: 2 minority students from Xinjiang + 8 Han students from Shanghai — all from the same education major, united by a shared vision of contributing to women's entrepreneurship and rural revitalization through rigorous field research.

Methodology

How We Researched

Method 01

Questionnaire Survey

Designed around four dimensions — Knowledge, Attitude, Intention, and Action — collecting 264 valid responses to map women's awareness and readiness for entrepreneurship.

Method 02

Stakeholder Interviews

Phone interviews with the deputy town mayor, Party branch secretary, and Women's Federation officials; combined with on-site visits for community-level insight into local conditions.

Method 03

Entrepreneur Case Studies

In-depth profiles of four exemplary entrepreneurial women — documenting their paths, obstacles, and the conditions that enabled their success in the local economy.

Method 04

Policy Analysis

Analysis of national and local frameworks — including the "Rural Revitalization Women's Action" directive — to situate field findings within the broader institutional and policy context.

Key Findings

What We Found

📊

Awareness Gap Despite General Endorsement

Women broadly endorsed the value of entrepreneurship, but knowledge of the "Rural Revitalization" policy framework remained low — a disconnect between top-down policy and grassroots understanding.

💡

Willingness Outpaces Action

Overall motivation to start a business was strong, but concrete entrepreneurial action was limited — pointing to a gap between intention and the practical resources, skills, and confidence needed to begin.

🎓

Training Misalignment

Existing vocational training programs did not match women's actual literacy levels or learning contexts. Content, instructors, and delivery formats were all misaligned with rural women's daily realities.

🏠

Structural and Cultural Barriers

Heavy household burdens, traditional gender norms, insufficient social support, and inadequate policy outreach converged to limit women's capacity to pursue entrepreneurship — even when the will was there.

Case Studies

Four Women, Four Stories

Through field research, we documented the journeys of four model entrepreneurs. Their stories revealed a consistent pattern: success required the convergence of personal resilience, government support, and the spirit locals call "马兰精神" (Malan Spirit) — named after the desert flower that blooms in harsh conditions.

01

Personal Drive

Each entrepreneur showed that breaking from passive expectations — despite family pressure — was the essential first step toward building something of their own.

Internal Factor
02

Policy Enablement

Government entrepreneurship incentives — loans, subsidies, and training access — provided the structural conditions that made individual ambition viable.

External Factor
03

Institutional Leadership

Active guidance from local government and the Women's Federation created a credible path forward and helped overcome institutional inertia in conservative communities.

Community Factor
04

Malan Spirit

This local ethos of resilience — named after the desert flower that thrives in harsh conditions — gave women cultural permission and collective encouragement to pursue their ambitions.

Cultural Factor

Recommendations

A Three-Level Framework

01

National Macro Policy

  • Strengthen multi-channel incentives for women's entrepreneurship
  • Leverage local media ecosystems to amplify success stories
  • Embed gender-responsive provisions in rural revitalization legislation
02

Inter-regional Aid (对口援疆)

  • Deepen sister-city partnership programs between Xinjiang and coastal cities
  • Channel resources into vocational training infrastructure
  • Connect rural entrepreneurs with broader market networks
03

Local Action

  • Expand evening schools and skills training bases in townships
  • Invite successful women entrepreneurs as community role models
  • Foster "四自" spirit: self-respect, self-confidence, self-improvement, self-reliance

Extension

The App We Designed

The research didn’t end on paper. Our team designed 巾帼创梦 · SheRise — a mobile app built specifically for the rural women we interviewed and surveyed. Rather than abstract policy, we gave them a tool: a platform that connects them with vocational training, entrepreneurship resources, and peer networks, designed around the real barriers our fieldwork uncovered.

🌸
巾帼创梦 · SheRise WeChat Mini Program  |  农村妇女创业学习平台

A mobile learning platform that helps rural women in Xinjiang acquire vocational skills and entrepreneurship knowledge — at their own pace, in their own language.

Agriculture, Livestock & Crafts modules — structured learning tracks across the three most viable industries for local women, with sub-modules like dragon fruit cultivation, livestock care, and handicraft production

Step-by-step video lessons — each topic broken into short units with video tutorials, illustrated guides, and progress tracking

Community forum (论坛) — peer discussion space where women share experiences, ask questions, and support each other across townships

Personal dashboard — tracks completion rates per module and surfaces recommended next steps based on learning history
